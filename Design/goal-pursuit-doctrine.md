# Goal pursuit & adaptive planning doctrine

**Author: David. Locked 2026-08-17. Canonical.**

> The goal doesn't move the workouts. The workouts move you toward the goal.
> And the app is constantly measuring whether they're actually doing it.

The app must never make either lazy mistake:

- *"You said 1:30, so we'll train you at 1:30 fitness."*
- *"You ran 1:41, so your goal is now 1:41."*

---

## 1 · Four numbers, never collapsed into one

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Goal** | What they want to run |
| **Demonstrated fitness** | What recent evidence says they could run today under comparable conditions |
| **Adjusted fitness** | What that performance *represents* after elevation, temperature, wind, pacing, execution, pre-race fatigue, and whether it was truly raced |
| **Projected fitness** | Where they appear to be heading by race day, given remaining weeks, volume, workout response, consistency and interruptions |

Adjust only when there is enough evidence to justify it.

## 2 · The goal creates a gap, not a pace prescription

Goal pace says what must *eventually* become sustainable. Current fitness says what *today's* workout is.
A 1:30 goal with 7:20 threshold fitness gets threshold work at 7:10-7:20, not 6:52. Training at a future
fitness level does not accelerate arriving at it; it converts threshold work into VO2 work and reduces
the total productive load the athlete can accumulate.

## 3 · Decompose the gap physiologically

Not just finish time. Aerobic capacity · threshold / critical speed · endurance (holding a high fraction
for the distance) · running economy · durability (late-effort deterioration) · **training capacity** —
how much useful work this athlete can currently absorb per week.

The answer to "how do we get from 1:41 to 1:30" is rarely "run harder." It is often "build 32 to 45 mi/wk
sustainably, keep most of it easy, increase threshold volume, make 6:50-7:00 cheaper."

## 4 · Diagnose the limiter before changing the plan

- **A · Aerobic ceiling too low.** HR high from early, pace never sustainable. → volume, threshold
  progression, protect easy, delay heavy race-specific work.
- **B · Speed exists, endurance doesn't.** 5K/10K predict the goal, the long race doesn't. → longer
  threshold intervals, longer aerobic runs, controlled quality in long runs, less VO2.
- **C · The result understates fitness.** Hills, heat, bad pacing. → *don't overreact*; state the
  course/condition-adjusted equivalent.
- **D · Carrying fatigue, not unfit.** Workouts were strong, race-week readiness was poor. → distinguish
  fitness from preparedness on that day. **A poor tune-up during heavy training must not destroy the
  fitness model.**

## 5 · Every workout is evidence, weighted, with confidence

Races are highly informative. A 20-minute threshold interval is useful. A fast Tuesday easy run is barely
evidence. Maintain a confidence model and speak in ranges:

> Estimated HM capability: 1:37-1:40 · confidence high

not `1:38:17`. **The latter is fake precision.**

## 6 · Two targets per block

**Fitness target** — what adaptation are we creating now. **Goal target** — what it must eventually
become. Early in a block goal pace barely appears; later it dominates.

## 7 · Progression toward goal pace

Build the engine → raise the ceiling → connect fitness to the event → prove durability → taper.
Goal pace is *earned* into the plan, never assumed into it.

## 8 · Goal feasibility is five states, not a binary

**Supported** · trajectory supports it.
**Reach** · plausible, requires continued improvement.
**Stretch** · evidence doesn't support it yet, but enough time remains to keep pursuing.
**Unlikely on current trajectory** · required rate of improvement has become unusual.
**Unsupported** · no responsible progression closes it before race day.

At Unsupported the coach does not secretly change the goal:

> 1:30 can stay the long-term goal. Based on the fitness you've demonstrated and the time remaining, it
> no longer makes sense as the primary target for this race.

Then propose primary / stretch / long-term.

## 9 · Push when the gap is closable

Realism is not conservatism. If threshold improves, drift falls, volume climbs, RPE stays controlled and
recovery is stable — **progress earns progression.** The plan is a feedback loop, not a document to defend.

## 10 · Push is not the same as harder

Levers: weekly volume · frequency · long-run duration · threshold duration · interval volume · pace ·
density (reduced recovery) · race specificity.

> A normal training app progresses pace because pace is easy to see. A good coach often progresses
> capacity first.

3×8 min → 3×10 min at the same effort → 2×15 min slightly faster is progression, even though nothing
visible got faster.

## 11 · Protect the ability to train tomorrow

Injury is multifactorial; rigid acute:chronic thresholds and a flat 10% rule are not strong enough to
govern this. Use tolerated volume, consistency, injury history, pain signals, workout response, recovery,
frequency and experience. The objective is **maximum productive training the athlete can repeatedly
absorb** — not maximum training.

## 12-13 · The trajectory chart

The central object: three lines over time — **goal**, **current fitness**, and **expected race-day range**.
The question it answers is not "what will I run" but **"is the gap closing quickly enough?"** And the coach
explains *why* the line moved:

> Your race prediction moved 90 seconds this month. Most of that came from stronger threshold sessions and
> better durability on long runs, not raw speed.

## 14 · Fitness updates often. Goals do not.

A bad week, a bad workout, illness, even a bad race: recalculate, but do not move the goal. A goal change
requires **sustained evidence that the gap is no longer realistically closable.** Equally, one great 5K
does not earn a faster goal.

> The coach should be adaptive without having the emotional stability of a crypto chart.

## 15 · Checkpoints

16 weeks out: baseline. 12: is general fitness progressing? 8: is the goal physiologically plausible?
4-6: can the specific race demands be demonstrated? 2-3: **stop asking what fitness might become and set
race strategy from what exists.** That last transition — training goal to race execution goal — matters.

## 16 · Race targets become bands

By race week, an aspiration becomes an execution strategy: **primary · aggressive · stretch.** Open around
the primary and let the athlete earn aggression later. Better runners pace more evenly; large positive
splits are associated with worse outcomes.

## 17 · Explain causality

After every block: what improved, what didn't, what caused us to change training, and what that means for
the goal. Otherwise plan changes just feel algorithmic.

## 18 · The control loop

goal → establish capability → quantify gap → diagnose limiter → select stimulus → execute → measure
response → separate signal from noise → update fitness model → recalculate trajectory → decide:

**Stay** (it's working) · **Progress** (adapted, add stimulus) · **Modify** (not producing the intended
response) · **Protect** (fatigue, pain, illness or conditions make progression a bad trade).

## The promise

Not *"give us 16 weeks and we'll get you to 1:30."* Training science cannot responsibly promise that.

> Tell us where you want to go. We'll continually work out the best training you can handle to move you
> toward it, show you whether the gap is closing, push when the evidence says you can handle more, and
> tell you when the numbers stop supporting the original target.
